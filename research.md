<section class="page-hero"><div class="container"><p class="eyebrow">Our research</p><h1>What does shared cytoplasm change?</h1><p>We study how an organism coordinates gene expression when the usual boundaries between cells disappear.</p></div></section>
<article class="container prose"><h2>Cell fusion is a developmental decision</h2><p>Syncytia are a recurring biological solution: they appear in muscle, placenta, fungal networks, and the <em>C. elegans</em> epidermis. But when cells fuse, gene regulation faces a new problem. Many nuclei now inhabit one continuous cytoplasm, where signals and gene products can travel beyond a single cell.</p><p>Our central question is how this architectural change influences developmental timing and cellular identity. Owen’s work showed that epidermal cell fusion promotes the transition from embryonic to larval transcriptomes in <em>C. elegans</em>. The lab will build from that insight to uncover the mechanisms that coordinate transcription across a syncytium.</p><h2>Three connected approaches</h2><h3>Regulatory logic of cell fusion</h3><p>We identify the factors that link fusion to developmental transcriptional transitions, from candidate regulators to genome-wide expression programs.</p><h3>Single-nucleus multi-omics</h3><p>We use single-nucleus RNA-seq and chromatin-accessibility profiling to map distinct nuclear states inside multinucleated tissues and ask how those states change over time.</p><h3>Developmental genetics in <em>C. elegans</em></h3><p>Worm genetics and imaging let us test causal models quickly, connecting regulatory observations to the behaviors that build an animal.</p><h2>Why this matters</h2><p>Understanding how nuclei coordinate across shared cytoplasm will illuminate a basic principle of multicellular life. It may also offer a useful framework for syncytial tissues and diseases in which cell architecture, gene regulation, and differentiation become uncoupled.</p></article>
